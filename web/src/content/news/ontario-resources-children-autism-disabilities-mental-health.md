:::lead
When a child needs support, the hardest part is often knowing where to start.
:::

A parent may be wondering whether their child needs an autism assessment. A caregiver may be trying to find respite before the family reaches a breaking point. A teacher may be encouraging a family to ask about school supports. A teenager may be struggling with anxiety, grief, self-harm thoughts, or big emotions that feel too heavy to carry alone.

Sometimes the need is connected to foster care. Often, it is not. Children may need help while living with parents, relatives, kin caregivers, foster caregivers, adoptive families, or other caring adults.

This guide is meant to give Ontario families and caregivers a practical starting point. It is not a complete list of every program, and it is not emergency advice. Programs, funding rules, and contact details can change, so it is always best to use the linked sources to confirm current information before applying or making decisions.

:::pullquote
You do not have to know the whole system before asking for help. You only need to find the next right door.
:::

## If there is immediate danger, start with safety

:::callout{title="In a crisis, start here"}
- **Immediate danger or a medical emergency:** call 9-1-1.
- **Thoughts of suicide:** call or text [9-8-8](https://988.ca/), any time, day or night.
- **Kids and teens:** [Kids Help Phone](https://kidshelpphone.ca/) at 1-800-668-6868, or text CONNECT to 686868.
- **Indigenous children, youth, and families:** [Hope for Wellness](https://www.hopeforwellness.ca/) at 1-855-242-3310, 24/7.
:::

If a child or youth is in immediate danger, needs urgent medical support, or may be at risk of serious harm, start with emergency help. The Government of Canada's [mental health support page](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/mental-health-services/mental-health-get-help.html) says to call 9-1-1 if someone is in immediate danger or needs urgent medical support.

If a child, youth, or adult is thinking about suicide, they can call or text [9-8-8: Suicide Crisis Helpline](https://988.ca/) at any time. The Government of Canada says 9-8-8 is available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week for people who are thinking about suicide or are worried about someone else.

Young people can also contact [Kids Help Phone](https://kidshelpphone.ca/) for 24/7 support across Canada. Kids Help Phone offers phone support at 1-800-668-6868, text support by texting CONNECT to 686868, online messaging, and other mental-health tools and resources.

For Indigenous children, youth, families, and adults, the [Hope for Wellness Helpline](https://www.hopeforwellness.ca/) is available 24/7 by phone at 1-855-242-3310 or online chat. Indigenous Services Canada describes Hope for Wellness as immediate help for all Indigenous people across Canada, including crisis intervention and emotional support.

For non-emergency mental-health support in Ontario, [One Stop Talk](https://onestoptalk.ca/) offers free, confidential, immediate mental-health support for children and youth under 18 with a registered therapist. It can be a helpful first step when a young person needs to talk to someone but the situation is not a 9-1-1 emergency.

No one needs to wait until a crisis is "bad enough" to reach out. Asking for support early can prevent a situation from becoming more serious.

## If you are not sure what service you need, start with 211

When the question is "Where do I even begin?", [211 Ontario](https://211ontario.ca/) is often one of the best first stops. 211 describes itself as a helpline that connects people to social services, programs, and community supports.

That matters because many children's services are local. A family in Toronto, Ottawa, Windsor, Sudbury, Thunder Bay, Hamilton, Peel, Durham, Simcoe, or a rural community may have different intake numbers, waitlists, respite options, children's treatment centres, mental-health walk-in clinics, developmental services, or parent-support programs.

You can use [211 Ontario's search page](https://211ontario.ca/search/) to look for services online, or contact 211 by phone, text, chat, or email. It can be especially helpful if you are looking for:

- disability supports;
- child and youth mental-health services;
- respite programs;
- parenting and family supports;
- financial assistance;
- housing or emergency help;
- food and basic needs;
- local autism or developmental-service programs;
- programs in a specific city, region, language, or cultural community.

When contacting 211 or any other intake service, it helps to have basic information ready: the child's age, city or region, main concern, diagnosis if there is one, school concerns, safety concerns, and what kind of help would make the biggest difference right now.

## For autism supports, start with the Ontario Autism Program and AccessOAP

For children and youth with an autism diagnosis, the main provincial program is the [Ontario Autism Program](https://www.ontario.ca/page/ontario-autism-program). The Government of Ontario says the program supports families of children and youth on the autism spectrum, and that children and youth with a written autism diagnosis from a qualified professional can receive services and supports until age 18.

Registration and program support go through [AccessOAP](https://accessoap.ca/), the Ontario Autism Program's independent intake organization. The Ontario Autism Program page explains that AccessOAP care coordinators can answer questions, help families understand service options, and help connect families with needs-based services and supports in the community.

The Ontario Autism Program includes several types of services and supports, including foundational family services, caregiver-mediated early years programs, core clinical services, the entry-to-school program, and urgent response services. Not every child will use every service, and eligibility can depend on age, need, program rules, and availability.

Families can also look to [Autism Ontario](https://www.autismontario.com/programs-services) for programs, webinars, regional events, learning resources, and parent or caregiver support groups. Autism Ontario says its regions run programming for autistic children, youth, and adults, as well as parent and caregiver support groups.

A useful first step is to write down what you are trying to solve. Is the child waiting for diagnosis? Already diagnosed and not yet registered? Registered but unsure what services are available? Struggling at school? Needing communication support, occupational therapy, behavioural support, caregiver coaching, or respite? The clearer the question, the easier it is for intake workers and care coordinators to point you in the right direction.

## For disabilities and complex needs, look at funding, service coordination, and local supports

Children with disabilities and complex needs may need support across several systems at the same time: home, school, health care, therapy, respite, transportation, equipment, behaviour support, communication, and family support.

The Government of Ontario's [children with special needs](https://www.ontario.ca/page/children-special-needs) page is a helpful starting place for provincial programs and services for children and youth with physical and/or developmental disabilities. It includes links to rehabilitation services, children's treatment centres, special needs resources in child care, autism supports, and other programs.

Two common Ontario funding programs are [Assistance for Children with Severe Disabilities](https://www.ontario.ca/page/assistance-children-severe-disabilities-program) and [Special Services at Home](https://www.ontario.ca/page/special-services-home).

Assistance for Children with Severe Disabilities, often called ACSD, provides financial support for eligible families to help cover some of the extra costs of caring for a child who has a severe disability. Special Services at Home, often called SSAH, helps families caring for a child with a developmental and/or physical disability and may help with costs related to respite, personal growth, and development.

These programs have eligibility rules and application processes. A family may qualify for one program, both, or neither. The most useful approach is to read the current program pages carefully, gather documentation, and ask a service provider, doctor, social worker, school staff member, or community agency for help if the forms feel overwhelming.

For children and youth with multiple or complex special needs, [coordinated service planning](https://www.ontario.ca/page/coordinated-service-planning-children-special-needs) may also help. Ontario describes coordinated service planning as a way for families to connect with a dedicated service planning coordinator when a child has multiple or complex special needs. The purpose is to help families bring services together instead of trying to manage every system alone.

For younger children, Ontario's [Infant and Child Development Program](https://www.ontario.ca/page/infant-child-development-program) serves children from birth to school entry who have a developmental disability or are at risk of developmental delay. The [Healthy Babies Healthy Children program](https://www.ontario.ca/page/healthy-babies-healthy-children-program) is also a free and voluntary program for families who are pregnant or have young children.

The right starting point depends on the child. A baby with developmental concerns may need early intervention. A school-aged child with physical and developmental needs may need a children's treatment centre, school supports, respite, and funding applications. A teenager with complex mental-health and developmental needs may need coordinated service planning, child and youth mental-health support, crisis planning, and transition planning.

The goal is not to find one perfect program. It is to build a circle of support that matches the child's real life.

## For child and youth mental health, start with one conversation

Mental-health concerns can show up in many ways. A child may become withdrawn, angry, anxious, tearful, aggressive, unusually quiet, unable to sleep, afraid to go to school, overwhelmed by transitions, or no longer interested in things they used to enjoy.

The Government of Ontario's [mental health services for children and youth](https://www.ontario.ca/page/mental-health-services-children-and-youth) page says there are hundreds of programs and services across Ontario for children and youth up to age 18 with mental-health and addiction concerns. That can be encouraging, but it can also feel overwhelming.

One simple first step is [One Stop Talk](https://onestoptalk.ca/), which provides free, confidential, immediate mental-health support for Ontario children and youth under 18. Another is [Children's Mental Health Ontario's Find Help tool](https://cmho.org/findhelp/). Children's Mental Health Ontario says its network of child and youth mental-health centres can connect families with child mental-health professionals, including psychiatrists, therapists, and psychologists. Its Find Help page also notes that centres may offer virtual, phone, in-person appointments, and walk-in clinics, and that families do not need a referral or OHIP card.

Kids and teens can also contact [Kids Help Phone](https://kidshelpphone.ca/) directly. Sometimes a young person will talk more easily to a confidential support service before they are ready to talk to a parent, caregiver, teacher, or doctor.

For caregivers, it can help to track what has changed: sleep, appetite, school attendance, friendships, hygiene, panic symptoms, anger, self-harm, substance use, grief, bullying, family changes, online safety concerns, or any mention of wanting to die. Specific examples are more useful than labels. "She cries for two hours before school three mornings a week" gives an intake worker more information than "She is anxious."

## For school concerns, ask about special education supports

Many children first show signs of needing support at school. A child may be falling behind, refusing school, having frequent meltdowns, struggling with communication, being sent home often, having trouble with transitions, or needing support with personal care, mobility, sensory needs, behaviour, attention, or learning.

In Ontario's publicly funded schools, families can ask the school about special education supports, an Individual Education Plan, and the Identification, Placement and Review Committee process. The Government of Ontario's [Special Education in Ontario policy and resource guide](https://www.ontario.ca/document/special-education-ontario-policy-and-resource-guide-kindergarten-grade-12) is written for school boards and schools, but it can help parents and caregivers understand the language schools use, including IEPs and IPRCs.

A practical starting point is to contact the child's teacher, principal, special education resource teacher, or school board special education staff. Put concerns in writing where possible. Ask what supports are already being used, what has been tried, what data the school is collecting, and whether an IEP, assessment, safety plan, transition plan, or case conference would be appropriate.

For children in care, school planning should also involve the child's worker and caregiver, and should consider placement stability, transportation, family contact, trauma, disability, culture, and the child's own voice where appropriate.

## For First Nations and Inuit children, ask about Jordan's Principle or the Inuit Child First Initiative

Some children may be eligible for supports through federal child-first programs.

[Jordan's Principle](https://www.sac-isc.gc.ca/eng/1568396042341/1568396159824) is for First Nations children. Indigenous Services Canada explains that Jordan's Principle is meant to make sure First Nations children do not face gaps or delays in accessing government services, or get denied services because of their identity as First Nations children. Examples of supports approved under Jordan's Principle have included speech therapy, educational supports, medical equipment, mental-health services, and more.

Requests for Inuit children can be made through the [Inuit Child First Initiative](https://www.sac-isc.gc.ca/eng/1536348095773/1536348148664). Indigenous Services Canada says the Inuit Child First Initiative supports Inuit children in accessing essential government-funded health, social, and educational products, services, and supports.

These programs are not emergency services. If there is a life-threatening emergency, call 9-1-1 or go to the nearest health facility. But when the issue is a service gap, funding barrier, delay, or support need, these programs may be important doors to ask about.

## If child welfare is involved, the same supports still matter

A child does not stop needing community, health, school, disability, autism, or mental-health supports because child welfare is involved. In fact, those supports may become even more important during periods of stress, separation, grief, trauma, or placement change.

For a child in foster care, kinship care, customary care, or another placement, the caregiver should usually involve the child's worker before making applications, sharing records, starting services, or consenting to assessments. The worker may need to help with documentation, approvals, funding, referrals, transportation, family involvement, cultural planning, or communication with the child's parents or legal guardians.

The practical question is still the same: what does this child need to be safe, well, connected, and included?

That may mean keeping therapy consistent after a move. It may mean making sure school supports follow the child. It may mean registering for autism services, applying for respite, asking about coordinated service planning, supporting sibling contact, or ensuring a child's culture and identity are not treated as an afterthought.

:::pullquote
Supports should follow the child, not the system.
:::

## What to write down before you call or apply

Before calling a helpline, school, program, intake service, or funding office, it can help to gather a short summary. You do not need a perfect file. Start with what you know.

Helpful information may include:

- the child's full name, age, and city or region;
- the main concern in plain language;
- any diagnosis, assessment, or suspected need;
- examples of what is happening at home, school, child care, or in the community;
- safety concerns, including self-harm, aggression, running away, unsafe caregiving stress, or medical risk;
- current doctors, therapists, schools, workers, or agencies involved;
- medications, equipment, communication tools, mobility needs, feeding needs, or allergies;
- school information, including IEPs, suspensions, attendance issues, transportation, or recent meetings;
- what services have already been tried;
- what is urgent now;
- what kind of help would make daily life safer or more manageable.

It is also worth keeping a simple log of calls and emails: date, person spoken to, agency, phone number or email, what was promised, next step, and any deadline. Families and caregivers often end up repeating the same story many times. A small record can make that easier.

## A simple first-week plan

If everything feels overwhelming, try thinking in short steps.

First, deal with safety. If there is immediate danger, call 9-1-1. If suicide is a concern, call or text 9-8-8. If a young person needs to talk, use Kids Help Phone or One Stop Talk.

Second, ask for navigation. Contact 211 Ontario, a family doctor, school, public health unit, children's treatment centre, child and youth mental-health centre, or another trusted local provider.

Third, match the child's needs to the right doorway. Autism may mean the Ontario Autism Program and AccessOAP. Complex special needs may mean coordinated service planning, children's treatment centres, ACSD, SSAH, or early intervention. Mental-health concerns may mean One Stop Talk, a local child and youth mental-health centre, Kids Help Phone, or a crisis service. School concerns may mean an IEP conversation, IPRC discussion, or school-based planning.

Fourth, ask what happens next. Is there a waitlist? Is there an intake form? Do you need a doctor's letter, diagnosis, proof of income, school information, custody documentation, or consent from a guardian? Is there anything available while you wait?

Fifth, keep the child at the centre. Programs matter, but the child is not a file. They are a person who needs adults to stay calm, persistent, and hopeful even when the system is hard to navigate.

## How this connects to Annie's Havens

Annie's Havens works with children and youth who may have many different needs. Some children need lower-needs care. Some need support related to developmental, physical, or medical needs. Some need trauma-focused care. Our [care programs](/care-programs) are built around the understanding that children need more than a bed. They need stability, relationships, planning, and supports that fit who they are.

That is why resource navigation matters.

Whether a child is living with family, kin, foster caregivers, or another caring adult, the right support can reduce stress, improve safety, and help the child participate more fully at home, at school, and in the community. No single article can solve the challenge of navigating Ontario's service systems, but a good starting point can make the next step feel less impossible.
